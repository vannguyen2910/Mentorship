## Overview

Desk Research left the mentee with evidence: a competitor pattern log, a pass through the other sources, an evaluation of the current experience, an updated Assumption Map, and a first attempt at insight statements. What it didn't answer is *why* any of it matters enough to the person living it. Evidence without motivation is what makes a stakeholder readout sound like a bug list instead of a reason to act.

This session closes that gap with Jobs to Be Done: the framework for understanding what a person is actually trying to accomplish, not who they demographically are. It's entirely new content for this audience, so it gets real teaching time, not a reference link. The session then runs the same discipline the mentee has already learned (target a belief, gather evidence, compare), but this time the belief being tested is the business's own assumption about what job the customer is hiring the product for. A short stakeholder check-in surfaces that assumption, using the Assumption Map skill the mentee already has rather than a new technique. A JTBD-informed customer interview, run more than once, tests it against what the customer actually says. The gap between assumed and actual is what the session builds into a working artifact.

The session opens with the stakeholders' belief and closes by turning the validated job into a JTBD map: stages across the top, evidence and pain stacked underneath, the 🔥 focus area marked where negative experience concentrates. Framing that map into a pitched insight (the three-leg formula: observation, implication, recommended decision) is next session's work. Problem Definition & Strategy picks up from exactly this map.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, mentees will be able to:

1. **Apply** the JTBD framework (the three job layers, the job statement formula, and the "so that" test for telling a job from an action) to build a stage-by-stage map of candidate job hypotheses for your own project, including where a single stage carries more than one live job
2. **Run** a stakeholder check-in that surfaces what the business assumes the customer's job is at each stage, starting from your own provisional guess and pointing the Assumption Map at one specific question rather than learning a new method
3. **Conduct** a JTBD-informed customer interview using the switch-interview timeline, across enough conversations to see a pattern rather than rely on one
4. **Derive** the actual job statement from that pattern, locate it on the stage map, and compare it against the stakeholder-assumed placement to find the gap between them
5. **Build** a JTBD map that turns the validated job into a working artifact: stages across the top, JTBD and subtasks per stage, expectation and positive/negative experience stickies, an opportunity for each pain point, and a marked 🔥 focus area
6. **Hand off** the map as the input the next session builds its insight and opportunity work on top of, rather than pitching a finding today

## Core Content

### Opening · From Data to Decisions

Last session produced evidence: evaluation findings, comparator patterns, an updated Assumption Map. Evidence answers "what's broken." It doesn't answer "why does this matter enough to the person experiencing it" or "why should a stakeholder change a decision because of this." Those are two different failures, and this session is built to close both: motivation first, persuasion second.

---

### Part 1 · Jobs to Be Done

#### What JTBD is

People don't hire a product because of who they are. They hire it to make progress on something specific, in a specific circumstance. Jobs to Be Done is the framework for finding that circumstance and that motivation, not a demographic description of the person.

**Full framework (the three job types, the job statement formula, the switch-interview technique, and how to derive an actual job from a pattern) lives in `Library/frameworks/jtbd/framework-jtbd.md`. Teach directly from that file.** What follows here is the condensed version plus this session's specific application.

Quick recap for the room:

                     | Type | Covers |
|---|---|
| Functional | The practical task being accomplished |
| Emotional | How the person wants to feel, or stop feeling |
| Social | How the person wants to be seen by others |

Formula: *"When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome]."*

#### Why it's a hypothesis, not a finding

A job statement written before talking to anyone is exactly as proven as an entry on the Assumption Map before it's tested, which is to say, not at all. Call it a **candidate job hypothesis** until a pattern across real interviews confirms it. This distinction matters more here than almost anywhere else in the curriculum: JTBD sounds authoritative when it's well-formatted, and that confidence is easy to mistake for evidence.

#### JTBD vs. persona

The fastest way to check a job statement: does it start with "when [situation]" or does it quietly start with "as a [type of person]"? The second one is a persona wearing JTBD language. Persona answers who; job answers why, in this specific circumstance; the same person can be hiring different jobs in different circumstances.

#### What JTBD looks like in action · breaking a job into stages

A job statement explains *why* someone is motivated. It doesn't say *where in their process* that motivation actually shows up, or how it shifts along the way; a recommendation is hard to scope without knowing which stage it belongs to. Break the job into its own stages, in your own words (however many the project actually has, typically 3 to 6, not a fixed universal count), and write a job statement for each stage.

**What actually defines a stage boundary.** A stage isn't an arbitrary label pulled from a generic funnel. It's exactly the circumstance from the job statement: *"when [circumstance], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome]."* As long as the circumstance holds, it's the same stage and the same job. The moment the circumstance changes enough that the motivation changes with it, that's a new stage, and it needs its own job statement. That's the test for where to cut a boundary: did the circumstance change, not did time pass.

**Telling a job from an action: the "so that" test.** JTBD phrasing collapses three different things into what sounds like one: the trigger (why someone entered the stage), the circumstance (what's true while they're in it), and the job (the underlying why of what they do inside it). Give the room a test: for anything that sounds like a job, ask "so that...?" If you can still add something new after it, you haven't reached the real job yet; you're still describing the action. Run it live: "enter delivery address," so that... the courier actually finds me. That's the real job; everything before it was still the action. A second example to have ready: "confirm order details," so that... I trust what I'm paying for.

**One stage can carry more than one live job.** A stage isn't guaranteed to reduce to a single job statement. Real example: food delivery, the checkout stage. Three genuine jobs live inside the same circumstance at once: confirm the order is right before paying, trust the payment is safe, and know when it'll arrive. Same circumstance the whole time (I'm checking out), but three different anxieties are live, so three job statements, not one. Don't force a stage into a single flat sentence if the evidence doesn't support it.

**Applied to the food-delivery job used throughout this session:** four stages, four genuinely different situations: browsing, ordering and checkout, waiting for delivery, receiving the order. The actual job ("let me stop worrying it's not coming") sits specifically at stage 3, waiting for delivery, not spread evenly across the whole thing. The job statement says that matters. The stage breakdown says exactly where. (The framework file's own worked example uses a different case, a home-loan application; full detail and its worked table: `Library/frameworks/jtbd/framework-jtbd.md`.)

This comes back in Part 4: once the actual job is derived from real interviews, it gets broken into its own stages to scope where the map and its focus area actually belong.

### Part 2 · Point Your Assumption Map at the Customer's Job

**Why this stage exists.** Everything on the map so far is what the team believes and what the business believes; nobody's confirmed any of it is true yet. The people funding and shipping the work carry their own belief about the customer, untested, the same way the mentee's own stage-map hypotheses are untested. Benefit: this surfaces where the business's belief diverges from what the mentee is finding, before that gap becomes a fight at the readout. Outcome: a marked-up stage map showing assumed vs. actual, which feeds straight into the interview and the map.

This isn't a second interview method to learn. It's the Assumption Map skill from Desk Research, aimed at one specific question: *what job does the business believe the customer is hiring us for, and why do they believe that?*

The switch-interview technique from Part 1 is built to reconstruct a real customer's decision journey; it doesn't transfer to a stakeholder who never switched to anything. Running it on an internal conversation would be a category error, not just a shorter version of the same thing. What a stakeholder conversation actually produces is an assumption, captured exactly the way the Assumption Map already teaches: WHO/WHAT/WHY/SIGNAL, sorted by importance and evidence.

#### Placing the assumed job on the stage map

Before the real check-in happens, place a provisional guess of the stakeholder's belief onto the stage map built in Part 1: which stage do they likely think the job matters most at, and does that match where the mentee's own hypotheses point? This isn't the confirmed answer yet, it's a rehearsal: a visible, specific gap to go test, instead of walking into the meeting with an open-ended "what do you think the job is." The stage where the guess and the hypothesis diverge most is the one worth listening for specifically once the real conversation happens.

**Questions to bring into the meeting:**

- "What job do you think the customer is doing here?"
- "Why do you believe that?"
- "What would change your mind?"

**Practical note:** this doesn't need a booked meeting of its own. Fold it into a 1:1, a standup, a check-in that's already on the calendar. Getting dedicated time with a PM or business owner inside the same window as everything else in this assignment is often the harder scheduling problem: solve it by attaching to something that already exists, not by adding a new invite.

### Part 3 · The JTBD-Informed Customer Interview

**Why this stage exists.** The stage map is still a hypothesis. Time to test it: so far it's team hypotheses and stakeholder beliefs, no customer has confirmed any of it yet. This is the session's primary method: where the actual job gets tested against real behaviour, not assumed belief. Benefit: it will confirm or correct the map before anything gets built on top of a guess. Outcome by the end of Part 3: a validated (or revised) JTBD map, backed by real quotes, ready to hand to design.

#### The switch timeline

Structure the conversation around the timeline of the decision, not a list of open questions about the product. Full detail in the framework file; the five stages: first thought, passive looking, active looking, deciding, using. Each stage has its own prompt: "when did you first start thinking about this," "what were you doing instead before you looked for something new," "what made today the day you actually went looking," "what almost stopped you," "is it doing what you hoped."

#### Scoping check · does this project even have a switch moment?

The switch-interview technique assumes a real decision point: someone was doing something else, then chose this instead. That fits a project with a genuine adoption or application moment: applying for a loan, opening an account, switching providers. It fits far less cleanly onto a workflow feature inside a process someone's already committed to, where there's no "switch," only a task. Before drafting an interview guide, name which one this project actually is. If there's no switch moment, adapt the timeline to task initiation instead: first thought about needing to do the task, what they tried first, what made them look for a better way, what they chose, whether it worked.

#### Why more than one

One conversation is a story about one person on one day. The job is what repeats. Two to three interviews showing the same circumstance-motivation-outcome shape is the minimum bar before treating it as an actual job rather than an anecdote; this is the same discipline as the desk research minimum from last session (2–3 comparators, not one).

**Real example.** Three interviews, same stage, two agree: "I kept refreshing the tracking screen because I was scared it had just stopped." "I checked the map every few minutes, worried the driver had given up." The third interview tells a genuinely different story: wanting a person on the line, not a tracking screen. That third one doesn't get promoted to "the job" just because it's vivid or recent; two matching accounts outweigh one striking one.

#### What the interview leaves you with

Once the interview pattern is derived, one row on the map is fully filled in: stage, hypothesis (Part 1), assumed job (Part 2), actual job (Part 3), and the gap between assumed and actual. Worked example: assumed "show me my order is on the way" vs. actual "let me stop fearing it's not coming," both located at the waiting-for-delivery stage. That gap is exactly what Part 4 turns into a working artifact.

### Part 4 · Mapping the Job

**Why this stage exists.** The validated job doesn't stay a sentence. It becomes a visual map, because that's the format a design or delivery team can actually act on, and the format the next two lessons build directly on top of: customer journey mapping and service blueprinting both start from exactly this map. Skipping this step means re-deriving the same structure twice.

#### Set the foundation

Before filling in any stage, write down what's already known at the top of the map. This is a recap, not new teaching; every field already exists somewhere in the mentee's project.

- **Business objective:** what the stakeholder check-in said actually matters to the business
- **Target user context:** the circumstance from the job statement, who and when
- **Goals & KPIs:** whatever the team already measures success by

The only new move is writing them at the top of the map so anyone reading it shares the same frame before they hit the first stage.

#### Anatomy of the map

Read it left to right. Stages across the top are the confirmed stages from Part 3, unchanged. Each stage gets:

- A JTBD + subtasks card: the job statement plus the concrete steps someone actually takes at that stage
- A screenshot or flow slot, if the experience already exists
- Sticky types stacked underneath: expectation (what the user assumes will happen), positive and negative experience (what helps versus what hinders), and opportunity (a potential fix for the negative one)

Multiple users or personas get their own row: same stages across the top, a different lived experience underneath.

#### Filled example

One real stage, filled in, using the food-delivery job. Stage: waiting for delivery. JTBD: "When my order is out for delivery, I want to know it's still coming, so I can stop fearing it's not." Subtask: check the tracking screen. Expectation: the app will warn me before I have to ask. Negative experience: the ETA slips with no explanation. Opportunity: push a proactive alert the moment the ETA changes, instead of a static countdown the user has to keep checking.

#### Find the focus area

Once every stage has its stickies filled in, scan the whole row for where negative experience stacks up highest: that's the 🔥 focus area. This isn't a new judgment call. It's the same stage the actual job pointed to back in Part 3, now visible on the map instead of buried in an interview note. Mark it: it should match what customers and stakeholders already told you, and it's the entry point for the next two lessons, customer journey mapping and service blueprinting.

Turning this map into a pitched insight, the three-leg formula of observation, implication, and recommended decision, and framing it for a room, is next session's work. Problem Definition & Strategy picks up from exactly this map.

## Activities

### 🗺️ Activity · Build Your Stage Map

**Type:** In-class · Real current-state findings + AI tool linked to your project folder **Time:** 9 min **Format:** Solo

1. **(2 min)** Name the stages your project's job actually goes through, in your own words. Most projects land somewhere between 3 and 6. There's no fixed count or fixed vocabulary; name what's actually there.
2. **(5 min)** Point your AI tool at your current-state findings and Assumption Map, already saved in your project folder: no need to re-explain the project. Ask it to (1) generate the stages, and (2) draft one or more candidate JTBD hypotheses per stage, using this format: *"When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome]"*, naming that a stage can hold more than one job. Output as `jtbd-map.md`, labelled draft.
3. **(2 min)** Check each stage's hypothesis against two tests: does it start with "when [situation]" rather than "as a [type of person]"? And does it survive the "so that...?" test: if you can still add something new after it, it's still an action, not the job yet. Rewrite anything that fails either one.
> **Critical check:** AI defaults to demographic language unless told not to. If a hypothesis reads like a persona with extra steps, that's the tell.

**Deliverable:** save the stage map to `jtbd-map.md` (markdown): one or more candidate JTBD hypotheses per stage, labelled draft, not yet validated.

---

### 🗣️ Activity · Prepare Your Stakeholder Check-in

**Type:** In-class · Stage map from the previous activity + Assumption Map template **Time:** 4 min **Format:** Solo

1. **(1 min)** Place your own guess for each stage: which one does the stakeholder likely think the job matters most at, and does that match your own hypothesis? Circle the stage with the biggest gap.
2. **(1 min)** Identify the meeting already on your calendar that this question can attach to.
3. **(2 min)** Draft 2–3 questions for that meeting, aimed at the stage where step 1 showed the biggest gap: what job do they think the customer is hiring you for at that point, why they believe that, what would change their mind. Optionally, ask your AI tool to draft the check-in plan: a goal for the conversation, the 2–3 questions aimed at the stage with the biggest gap, what answer would confirm vs. break each one, and how to log it.

**Deliverable:** the check-in questions saved as a new entry on your existing `assumption-map.md` (markdown): same WHO/WHAT/WHY/SIGNAL formula, ready to log the real answer once the meeting happens.

---

### 📋 Activity · Draft Your JTBD-Informed Interview Guide

**Type:** In-class · AI tool linked to your project folder + framework file **Time:** 10 min **Format:** Solo

1. **(1 min)** Name whether your project has a real switch moment or needs the task-initiation adaptation from Part 3.
2. **(2 min)** Take your stage map. Write one prompt for each stage of the timeline (first thought, passive/active looking, deciding, using) in your own words.
3. **(5 min)** Point your AI tool at your `jtbd-map.md`, already saved. Ask it to generate 8–10 interview questions along the switch timeline that test these specific stage hypotheses, not generic questions. Each should invite a story, not a yes/no answer.
4. **(2 min)** Cut to your strongest 5–6. Flag any that are really asking someone to explain their own motivation directly; rewrite those as timeline questions instead.

**Deliverable:** save to `interview-guide.md` (markdown).

## AI in Practice · Extend Your Audit Toolkit

Three reusable assets came out of Desk Research, plus `evaluation.md` from Evaluate Current Experience. This session adds another, following the same rule: extend the existing file, don't regenerate it.

### 🤖 The fifth asset

**`jtbd-map.md`**: new today, markdown, living and append-only, same as `assumption-map.md` and `competitor-pattern-log.md`. Four parts, built in order across this session and the assignment: (1) the stage map: the project's own stages, one or more candidate JTBD hypotheses per stage, drafted first; (2) the stakeholder's assumed job, placed against those same stages as a provisional guess today, then replaced with the confirmed answer once the real check-in happens; (3) the actual job, derived post-interview from the pattern across real conversations, plus the gap against the assumed placement; (4) the full map itself, built as a Figma or FigJam (or Miro) board from this markdown source: every stage, JTBD + subtasks, the sticky types, and the marked 🔥 focus area. A "linked insight" field stays a placeholder this session; it gets filled in properly next session, once Problem Definition & Strategy teaches the three-leg formula it takes to turn a gap into something a stakeholder can act on.

### 🧠 Critical thinking

- Where did your candidate job hypothesis survive contact with a real interview, and where did it turn out to be your own assumption in JTBD clothing?
- After comparing assumed vs. actual: which was harder to get right, running the interview, or resisting the urge to call one good interview "the answer"?

### ✍️ Prompt engineering tip

Your AI tool already has this project's files. After each interview, save the raw notes into the folder and ask AI to cross-check them against `jtbd-map.md`: *"Does this interview support, contradict, or complicate the stage hypotheses in my jtbd-map? Quote the specific line that makes you say so."* Check after every interview, not just once you think you're done: it's cheaper to catch a hypothesis drifting after interview 1 than after interview 3. There's no need to re-paste anything each time; it's already sitting in the same folder.

### ⚖️ Ethics consideration

A well-formatted job statement or map is not the same as a validated one. A JTBD map built on genuine evidence and one built on confident AI phrasing can read identically on the board; the difference only shows up if someone asks how many people you actually talked to.

Run verbally at the close of Part 4 (2–3 min):

1. What's the difference between a job statement and a persona?
2. Why is a job statement written before any interview still just a hypothesis?

## Assignments

### Assignment · From Assumed Job to a Working Map

**Due:** Before next session **Time estimate:** 3–4 hours, spread across 1–2 weeks to allow for real scheduling **Type:** Research + build · Your real project

1. Run the stakeholder check-in inside the meeting you already identified. Log the assumed job on your Assumption Map, and replace your draft placement in `jtbd-map.md` with what they actually said.
2. Conduct 2–3 JTBD-informed customer interviews using your interview guide. Not one: the pattern is the point. If a real scheduling blocker makes it truly impossible this cycle, say so explicitly and label the result "assumed" rather than "actual," since it's still coming from the stakeholder, not the customer.
3. After each interview, check it against your stage-map hypotheses (see the AI in Practice prompt engineering tip). Derive the actual job statement from what repeats across all of them: the job is what at least 2 of 3 people said.
4. Update your stage map in `jtbd-map.md` with what the real interviews actually showed: confirm, revise, or add stages where the draft from this session guessed wrong.
5. Update `jtbd-map.md`: stage-by-stage hypotheses, confirmed stakeholder-assumed job, actual job, the gap, and the confirmed stage breakdown.
6. Build the full JTBD map from Part 4's template: every stage, both users where relevant, JTBD + subtasks, every sticky type (expectation, positive, negative, opportunity), and the marked 🔥 focus area, as a board in Figma or FigJam (or Miro), plus the markdown source.

**Deliverable:** updated `jtbd-map.md` (markdown) and the JTBD map itself as a Figma or FigJam (or Miro) board. Share before the next session.

> **AI in Practice:** Point your AI tool at your saved interview notes files and your `jtbd-map.md`, already in your project folder: no need to paste any of it in again. Draft the actual job statement from that pattern, and flag anything that only showed up in one interview so I don't treat it as confirmed."*
