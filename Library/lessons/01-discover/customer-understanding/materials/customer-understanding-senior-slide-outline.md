### STATEMENT · The core claim
- Kicker: Reframe
- Title: People don't buy\nproducts. They *hire*\nthem for a job.
- On-slide: Caption only: "Different people. Same job." Small "the job" target/checkmark icon.
- Speaker notes: A 25-year-old and a 55-year-old can hire the exact same product for the exact same job. Two people who look identical on a persona slide can be hiring completely different jobs. JTBD explains motivation: not who someone is, but what they're trying to get done, and why they reached for a solution to do it.
- 🎨 Visual hint: White background, grid 1.1fr/1fr. Two silhouette icons pointing at one shared "job" target icon.

### NUMBERED · Three job layers
- Kicker: Framework
- Title: Every job carries\nthree layers.
- On-slide: 3 labels, one short quoted example each:
  - **Functional**: "Get this task done."
  - **Emotional**: "Feel confident, not anxious."
  - **Social**: "Be seen as in control."
- Speaker notes: Most real jobs are functional, emotional, and social all at once. A functional-only reading usually misses why the friction actually hurts. Full examples to walk through live: functional: "Get my order to me while it's still hot"; emotional: "Feel confident I haven't been forgotten"; social: "Be seen as someone who has this under control, not someone anxiously refreshing an app." Full framework file: `Library/frameworks/jtbd/framework-jtbd.md`.
- 🎨 Visual hint: White background. 3 stacked cards, each with a checkmark-style icon + bold label + short on-slide phrase.

### STATEMENT · The job statement formula
- Kicker: Write it right
- Title: Start with the\n*situation*, not\nthe person.
- On-slide: Formula only: *"When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome]."*
- Speaker notes: Circumstance-first, not persona-first: that's what separates a job statement from a persona line. Weak example: "As a busy professional, I want a fast checkout." Strong example: "When I'm waiting for my food delivery and the ETA just changed, I want to know exactly what's happening, so I can stop refreshing the tracking screen every minute out of anxiety it's not coming." Point out live: the weak one opens with "as a," the strong one opens with "when."
- 🎨 Visual hint: Soft lavender `#F6F3FB` background. One large formula card, each bracket term colour-coded distinctly. No weak/strong example on-slide; deliver the contrast verbally from notes.

---

### NUMBERED · JTBD vs persona
- Kicker: The trap
- Title: Persona answers who.\nJob answers *why,\nright now.*
- On-slide: Two-column labels only:
  - **Persona**: demographics, stable across products
  - **JTBD**: circumstance, stable only for this job
- Speaker notes: Quick test to give the room: does your statement start with "when [situation]" or does it secretly start with "as a [type of person]"? The second one is a persona in disguise. Same person can hire different jobs in different circumstances; that's the whole point of circumstance over identity.
- 🎨 Visual hint: White background. Two columns side by side, no footer text on-slide. Deliver the quick test verbally.

---

### STATEMENT · What defines a stage
- Kicker: Where stages come from
- Title: A stage is a\n*circumstance.* Change the\ncircumstance, get a new stage.
- On-slide: The formula again with "circumstance" highlighted: *"When [circumstance], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome]"*, plus a two-line contrast: "Same circumstance → same stage, same job statement" / "Circumstance shifts → new stage, write a new job statement." Footer line: "The cut test: did the circumstance change, not did time pass."
- Speaker notes: Bridge slide: a stage isn't an arbitrary label, it's exactly the circumstance from the JTBD statement. The job format is "when [circumstance], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome]." As long as the circumstance holds, it's the same stage and the same job. The moment the circumstance changes enough that the motivation changes with it, that's a new stage, and it needs its own job statement. That's the test for where to cut a stage boundary: did the circumstance change, not did time pass.
- 🎨 Visual hint: White background. Formula card at top, two contrast rows underneath (same circumstance vs. shifted circumstance), small footer callout for the cut test.

### DIAGRAM · The "so that" test
- Kicker: The test
- Title: Ask *"so that…?"*\nIf it opens onto\nsomething new, it\nwasn't the job yet.
- On-slide: Two worked examples, arrow flow:
  - "Enter delivery address" → so that… → the courier actually finds me
  - "Confirm order details" → so that… → I trust what I'm paying for
  - Footer: "If 'so that' still adds something new, keep asking, you're not at the job yet."
- Speaker notes: Teach the layers before the example: trigger (why they entered the stage), circumstance (what's true while they're in it), and job (the underlying why of what they do inside it) keep collapsing into one thing because JTBD phrasing sounds the same for all three. Give students the test: for anything that sounds like a job, ask "so that…?" If you can still add something new after it, you haven't reached the real job, you're still describing the action. Run "enter delivery address" through it live: enter delivery address, so that... the courier actually finds me. That's the real job.
- 🎨 Visual hint: White background. Two horizontal arrow-flow rows, action → "so that…" → real job, stacked. Footer callout beneath.

### DIAGRAM · Stage map
- Kicker: What it looks like in action
- Title: The job says *why.*\nStages say *where.*
- On-slide: generic placeholder chain, no real labels: Stage 1 → Stage 2 (2 JTBDs stacked, showing a stage can hold more than one) → Stage 3 → ⋯ → Stage N
- Speaker notes: A job statement explains motivation. It doesn't say where in the process that motivation shows up, or how it shifts along the way, and a recommendation is hard to scope without knowing which stage it belongs to. There's no fixed universal vocabulary here: say that explicitly, since a Job Map with fixed step names exists in the wild (Ulwick's Outcome-Driven Innovation) and this session deliberately trades that fixed scaffold for one that flexes per project. Walk the applied example live: the food delivery job broken into 4 stages (browsing, ordering and checkout, waiting for delivery, receiving the order), each with its own job statement. The actual job derived earlier, "let me stop worrying it's not coming," sits specifically at stage 3, waiting for delivery, not spread across the whole thing. That's the payoff: the statement says it matters, the stage breakdown says exactly where.
- 🎨 Visual hint: White background. Horizontal node chain, Stage 2 shown with two stacked JTBD tags to reinforce the "one stage, many jobs" idea just taught. Dashed/lighter styling signals "your stages, your words," not a fixed template.

### STATEMENT · Hypothesis, not job
- Kicker: Say this out loud
- Title: "Until it's tested,\nit's a *hypothesis*,\nnot a job."
- On-slide: small label under the quote: "Candidate job hypothesis"
- Speaker notes: A job statement written before talking to anyone has the same evidence status as an unproven entry on the Assumption Map. JTBD sounds authoritative when it's well-formatted; that confidence is easy to mistake for evidence. Label it a candidate job hypothesis until a pattern from real interviews confirms it. This is the single most important framing line of the session: say it slowly.

### STATEMENT · Why this stage exists
- Kicker: Why this stage exists
- Title: This is what stakeholders\n*believe.* You don't know\nyet what's *true.*
- On-slide: three short labelled lines:
  - **Why:** the people funding and shipping the work carry their own belief about the customer, untested
  - **Benefit:** surfaces the gap between assumed and actual before it becomes a fight at the readout

- Speaker notes: Land why before how. The customer interview tests your stage map against real customers; the stakeholder check-in tests it against the people who fund and ship the work. ### STATEMENT · Not a new method
- Kicker: Reuse, don't reinvent
- Title: This is your\nAssumption Map,\naimed at *one question.*
- On-slide: the one question only: "What job does the business believe the customer is hiring us for?" Plus the 2×2 Assumption Map matrix (importance × confidence), ghosted, with a new sticky labelled "Assumed customer job."
- Speaker notes: The switch-interview technique reconstructs a real customer's decision journey. A stakeholder never switched to anything; running that technique on them is a category error, not a shorter version of the same thing. What this conversation actually produces is an assumption. Capture it the way the mentee already knows how: WHO/WHAT/WHY/SIGNAL, same matrix as last session.

### DIAGRAM · Guess on the map
- Kicker: Before the real meeting
- Title: Guess first.\nThen go check it.
- On-slide: the same generic stage chain from Part 1 (Stage 1 → Stage 2? → Stage 3 → ⋯ → Stage N), one node marked with a small "?" tag
- Speaker notes: Before the real check-in happens, place a provisional guess of the stakeholder's belief onto the stage map from Part 1: which stage do they likely think the job matters most at, and does that match the hypotheses on the map? This isn't the confirmed answer, it's a rehearsal: a specific gap to go test instead of walking in with an open-ended "what do you think the job is." Whichever stage shows the biggest gap between guess and hypothesis is the one to listen for specifically once the real conversation happens.

### NUMBERED · Questions to bring
- Kicker: In the room
- Title: Three questions.\nOne meeting you\nalready have.
- On-slide: 3 short prompts only:
  1. "What job do you think the customer is doing here?"
  2. "Why do you believe that?"

### STATEMENT · Why this stage exists
- Kicker: Why this stage exists
- Title: Your stage map is\nstill a *hypothesis.*\nTime to test it.
- On-slide: three short labelled lines:
  - **Why:** so far it's team hypotheses and stakeholder beliefs, no customer has confirmed any of it yet

### DIAGRAM · The switch timeline
- Kicker: The primary method
- Title: Reconstruct the\ndecision. Don't ask\nfor the reason.
- On-slide: 5 stage names only (no prompts written out):
  - First thought → Passive looking → Active looking → Deciding → Using
- Speaker notes: People rationalise when asked "why did you do that?" directly. The switch interview reconstructs the timeline instead. Prompts to deliver verbally per stage: "When did you first start thinking about this?" / "What were you doing instead, before you looked for something new?" / "What made today the day you actually went looking?" / "What almost stopped you?" / "Is it doing what you hoped?"
- 🎨 Visual hint: White background. Horizontal 5-node pipeline, connected by arrows, "Deciding" as the visual pivot point. No prompt subtext on the nodes themselves.

### STATEMENT · Scoping check
- Kicker: Before you draft
- Title: Does this project\nhave a *switch*\nmoment, or a task?
- On-slide: two labels only: "Switch moment" / "Task-based"
- Speaker notes: The switch timeline assumes a real decision point: someone was doing something else, then chose this instead. That fits an application or adoption moment. It fits far less cleanly onto a workflow feature inside a process someone's already committed to. Name which one this is before drafting your guide; if there's no switch, adapt the timeline to task initiation instead: first thought about needing to do the task, what they tried first, what made them look for a better way, what they chose, whether it worked.
- 🎨 Visual hint: Soft lavender `#F6F3FB` background, grid 1fr/1fr. Two-path fork diagram, simple decision-tree shape, no extra text.

### STATEMENT · Why more than one
- Kicker: Sample size
- Title: One interview is\na story. The job is\nwhat *repeats.*
- On-slide: "2–3 interviews minimum" as the only supporting text
- Speaker notes: Two to three interviews showing the same circumstance-motivation-outcome shape is the minimum before calling it a job rather than an anecdote (same discipline as the 2–3 comparator minimum from last session's desk research). Worth naming directly: this is the moment a senior/lead gets tested on discipline, not skill; it's tempting to stop at one great interview.

### DIAGRAM · Real example · sample size
