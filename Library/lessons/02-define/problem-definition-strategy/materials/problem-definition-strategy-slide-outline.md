### COMPARISON · Two maps
- Kicker: Part 1 · The distinction
- Title: You already have one\nmap. This is a *different\nkind of map.*
- On-slide: two-column table, 4 rows only: Question / Spine / Whose it is / Ranking. Full table lives in the framework file, not on the slide.
- Speaker notes:
  - **JTBD map:** the customer's world as it is. Spine is their own stages. You're recording it. No ranking, every stage described equally.
  - **Opportunity map:** the team's decision space. Spine is one product outcome. You're defending it. Ranking is the entire point.
  - Why both: a JTBD map has no target, so nothing inside it can say which stage matters most.
  - Importance is a property of the business goal, not of the customer's journey.
  - Keep them as separate files. Rank stages by business value and you've stopped describing and started arguing for a plan.
  - You want to change the plan next quarter without rewriting your account of the customer.
- 🎨 Visual hint: Two side-by-side map sketches. LEFT: a horizontal chain of 4 stage boxes labelled browsing, ordering, waiting, receiving, --ink. RIGHT: an inverted tree, one node branching into four, --sienna. Chain versus tree carries the point.

### TWO-COLUMN · But my map already has opportunities
- Kicker: Part 1 · The objection
- Title: Those stickies are real.\nThey're just *solutions.*
- On-slide: The sticky as written: "Push a proactive alert the moment the ETA changes." Below, the test: **Name three different ways.** Then the rewrite: "I need to know the app will tell me the moment something changes, without going to look." Then three: a proactive alert · a promise up front about when we'll contact you · a courier message channel.
- Speaker notes:

  - Run the three-solutions test live, on their sticky, not mine.

  - Lifting each one a level *is* the exercise. Makes Build 1 a judgment task, not a clerical one.

### TWO-COLUMN · The root, and the focus area
- Kicker: Part 1 · The root
- Title: The root is a behaviour.\nThe 🔥 focus area is\n*something else.*
- On-slide: LEFT "Root outcome" / "More customers get through waiting-for-delivery without checking the tracking screen to reassure themselves." / "Where value moves. A fact about the business." RIGHT "🔥 Focus area" / "Where negative experience stacked highest." / "Where pain concentrates. A fact about the customer." Footer, struck through: "Increase order frequency 15%" · "Launch the new tracking screen" · "Improve the delivery experience"
- Speaker notes:
  - Root comes from the actual job statement, restated as a behaviour. Not from the business, which is chasing a number.
  - Test their business objective against the three struck-through shapes:
    - a metric can't be a root, nothing below it can be judged against it
    - an output is a solution wearing a target's clothes
    - an unfalsifiable phrase names no behaviour, so nothing can fail
  - **Then the question that matters:** if the behavioural root came true, would the business number move?
  - A "no" gets written into the map. Don't smooth it over.
  - Focus area is a third thing: it marks pain, not value.
  - On this map they agree (waiting-for-delivery is both). When they disagree, that's the most interesting thing they'll take to a stakeholder this quarter.
  - **Say plainly that this root is a proposal.** An outcome is committed to by a team, not derived by design and announced.
  - A root nobody agreed to is a target that doesn't exist. Getting it agreed is part of the assignment.
- 🎨 Visual hint: Two markers dropping onto the same four-stage chain from opposite sides: from above a --sienna pin labelled "root", from below a 🔥 marker in --ochre. Both land on stage 3 here. Struck-through rejected roots in a smaller footer row.

### NUMBERED · Three reframing practices
- Kicker: Part 1 · Reframing
- Title: Reframing generates\n*options.* It doesn't find\nthe real problem.
- On-slide: 3 labels, one line each:
  - **Question the objective**: "Whose goal is this, and what if it's wrong?"
  - **Positive exceptions**: "Who already succeeds, and what's different about them?"
  - **What's missing**: "What does this framing leave out of frame?"
- Speaker notes:
  - They now have a tree. This is an operation performed on it, which is why it's taught here and not earlier.
  - Not finding the real problem. Seeing whether there's a *better* problem.
  - Strongest branch only. Reframe everything and you get a philosophy seminar instead of a decision.
  - **Lead with positive exceptions:** it can't be done without stage-level data, so it stays tied to evidence.
  - Third practice if there's time: nobody has looked at what the courier sees, or what the restaurant knows first.
- 🎨 Visual hint: Three numbered cards. Card 2 emphasised in --sienna, the other two --ink, signalling which to lead with.

## 4. Part 3 · The Strategy

### STATEMENT · Part intro
- Kicker: Part 3
- Title: THE STRATEGY
- On-slide: nothing else
- Speaker notes:
  - The problem statement said what's wrong, who it's costing, how you know.
  - It didn't say what the team will do, what it'll refuse to do, or what winning looks like.

### PROCESS · The kernel
- Kicker: Part 3 · Structure
- Title: Three parts. Skip one\nand it isn't a *strategy.*
- On-slide: 3 nodes in a horizontal flow: **Diagnosis** "what's critical here" → **Guiding policy** "our approach" → **Coherent action** "what we'll do, and won't". Footer, smaller, four labels growing heavier left to right: "A pain on the map" → "an opportunity" → "an insight, then a statement" → "the diagnosis, unchanged"
- Speaker notes:
  - **Diagnosis:** a judgment naming what's critical. Not a provable fact.
  - **Guiding policy:** directional, not a task list.
  - **Coherent action:** moves that reinforce each other, not a list.
  - Today: diagnosis is the statement they just wrote, policy is three principles, actions are the moves plus the non-goals.
  - **Then point at the footer and make the join explicit.** One understanding, four shapes, no rewriting between the last two. It's what stops the session feeling like four disconnected exercises.
  - **If they start rewriting the diagnosis in Build 3:** either the statement wasn't finished in Build 2, or they've changed their mind about the problem. Stop for both.
- 🎨 Visual hint: Horizontal flow, three numbered circles connected by arrows, --sienna, two-word label under each. Below it, a thin footer band holding the four-stage chain in smaller type, identical wording, each step in progressively heavier weight.

### TWO-COLUMN · Bad strategy in a design deck
- Kicker: Part 3 · What to avoid
- Title: Most design strategy\nslides are *goals, actions,\nor fluff.*
- On-slide: LEFT (three struck lines): "Increase order frequency 15%" → a goal. "Redesign the tracking screen" → an action. "Be delightful" → fluff. RIGHT: "Each is missing the diagnosis."
- Speaker notes:
  - Be blunt. This is the shape of a real design strategy slide.
  - **Then bring in the overruled decision they brought from prep** (the last design call of theirs that got overruled). Almost always: the other side had a diagnosis and design had a preference.
  - **Hold onto this example.** It comes back in Part 4, replayed as a drill rather than just told.
- 🎨 Visual hint: LEFT column three struck-through lines in muted --ink. RIGHT a single --sienna block naming the missing piece. Asymmetric split, roughly 60/40.

### NUMBERED · Principles as decision rules
- Kicker: Part 3 · Guiding policy
- Title: Three to five. Each one\nhas to settle an *argument.*
- On-slide: **The test: say the credible opposite.** Then two examples: "Be delightful" / opposite "Be tedious" ✕ · "Say something even when nothing has changed" / opposite "Only interrupt when there's real news" ✓
- Speaker notes:
  - This is the guiding policy layer, so it inherits the job: directional, not a task list.
  - A principle earns its place only if it settles an argument between two reasonable options.
  - **The check:** say its credible opposite out loud. Obviously stupid opposite means the principle is decoration.
  - **Dwell on example 2.** It's the principle this diagnosis forces, and it costs something in notification noise.
  - That cost is what a guiding policy feels like when it's working.
  - Third example if there's time: "tell them before they ask" vs "answer well when they ask, invest in the tracking screen."
- 🎨 Visual hint: The test as a single line across the top. Below it two balance-scale motifs side by side: LEFT tipping absurdly with an ✕, RIGHT balanced evenly with a ✓ in --sienna.

---

### TWO-COLUMN · Actions are directions
- Kicker: Part 3 · The discipline
- Title: An action says where\neffort goes. Not *what\nto build.*
- On-slide: LEFT, ✓ "Confirm every state change to the customer as it happens." → a direction. RIGHT, ✕ "Send a push notification when the ETA slips." → a design. Below, spanning: **Every action carries one line: the riskiest assumption behind it.** Example: "assumes they want more messages, not fewer."
- Speaker notes:
  - **This is the coherent-action layer of the kernel** — where the guiding policy from the last slide turns into actual moves.
  - Easiest place in the session to break the discipline the session is teaching. Be explicit.
  - An action says where effort goes and where it stops. A design says what gets built.
  - Design belongs to the next stage, where alternatives get compared and the choice gets tested.
  - **Call the trap by name:** a mentee who writes three features here has done exactly what they rejected an hour ago when they lifted "push a proactive alert" off their own board.
  - **One line per action: the riskiest assumption.** It tells the next stage what to test first.
  - It also stops the brief reading as certainty when it's a bet. A strategy with no named risk isn't more confident, it's less honest.
- 🎨 Visual hint: Two columns. LEFT --sienna with a ✓, an arrow pointing forward along a road. RIGHT muted --ink with a ✕, a finished screen shape. Below both, spanning the width, a thin band holding the risk line in --ochre.

### STATEMENT · Non-goals
- Kicker: Part 3 · Commitment
- Title: If nobody could be\nannoyed by it, it isn't\na *non-goal.*
- On-slide: Subhead under the title, small: "Not everything you're not doing, just what's plausible enough that someone will assume it's included." Then a boundary line: above it, small and muted, "Everything else stays in scope." Below it, prominent, the sharp one: "We're not building a courier chat channel this quarter, even though one interview asked for it." Then, smaller, one that fails: "We're not opening our own restaurants." ✕
- Speaker notes:
  - **Still coherent action, the flip side of the last slide.** An action says where effort goes; a non-goal says where it explicitly doesn't.
  - A non-goal isn't everything you're not doing.
  - It's the plausible, attractive, adjacent work someone will otherwise assume is included.
  - The good one here names the rejected reframe and ties it to its evidence count.
  - That's exactly the line someone in the room will want to argue with. Good.
  - This is what turns a brief from a description into a commitment, and it's the part that gets quietly deleted in a deck.
- 🎨 Visual hint: A boundary line across the slide. Above it, small and muted in --ink: "Everything else stays in scope." Below it in --sienna, the named non-goals, fully legible rather than faded: deliberately visible, not hidden.

### COMPARISON · The target state, and how you'd know
- Kicker: Part 3 · What good looks like
- Title: Say it twice. Then\nsay which number is\n*yours.*
- On-slide: TOP, two panels sharing one outline: LEFT "In words" / "The customer puts the phone down after ordering, because the last thing the app told them was enough. When something changes, they hear it from us before they think to look." RIGHT "In numbers" / "Share of orders where the customer never opens the tracking screen after confirmation." BELOW, a compact 3-level ladder: **Business metric** "influenced" / **Product outcome** "owned — the number above" / **Output** "not evidence of anything". Footer, smaller: "No dashboard? Name a proxy and call it one: 'where is my order' contacts per 100 orders · a two-week tally · five follow-up calls, one question."
- Speaker notes:
  - **This is the piece named at the start of Part 3, on top of the three-part kernel.** Diagnosis, guiding policy and coherent action are all set. None of the three says what it looks like when it worked.
  - That's what a stakeholder pictures when deciding whether to fund it.
  - **In words:** the customer's terms, not the system's. Not "a proactive alerting service exists."
  - **In numbers:** the same sentence, stated so it can move. A vision with no number is a mood, a number with no vision is an accounting target.
  - **Then place that number on the ladder, immediately, same breath:** three levels, design owns exactly one.
  - **Business metric** (order frequency): influenced by design, owned by the business.
  - **Product outcome:** the behaviour design moves — it's the number they just wrote above.
  - **Output** (the alert shipped): evidence of activity, not of anything working.
  - No analytics isn't a reason to skip the number. Skipping it is what lets a bet never get evaluated. Name a proxy and call it one, and keep the word "proxy" in the stakeholder version.
- 🎨 Visual hint: Top: two panels sharing one outline with no dividing gap, LEFT a paragraph block in --sienna, RIGHT the same shape as a single large numeral with a small trend arrow. Below: three thin horizontal bands, MIDDLE band --sienna and wider with a small ownership marker pointing up at the numeral above it. Three small icons in --sienna beneath for the proxy options.

### COMPARISON · One finished brief
- Kicker: Part 3 · Before you write yours
- Title: You've seen every part.\nHere they are *together.*
- On-slide: the five parts of the food-delivery brief, compressed to one line each: **Diagnosis** "customers in waiting-for-delivery need to trust they'll be told when something changes... 3 of 3 interviews... it keeps generating 'where is my order' contacts" · **Guiding policy** "tell them before they ask · say something even when nothing has changed · design for the person waiting, not the parcel moving" · **Actions, with risks** "confirm every state change *(risk: they want fewer messages)* · commit to when we'll next make contact rather than a countdown *(risk: a promise we can't keep is worse than silence)* · stop investing in the tracking screen as the reassurance surface *(risk: it does more work than interviews showed)*" · **Non-goals** "not browsing or checkout this quarter · not a courier chat channel" · **Target state** "puts the phone down after ordering / share of orders with zero tracking-screen opens"
- Speaker notes:
  - **Do not send them into 16 minutes of writing without showing one complete example of the thing.**
  - Read it top to bottom in under a minute. They've seen every part already, just never together.
  - **Point at three things while it's up:**
    - every line traces back to something on their JTBD map, not to a good idea someone had
    - the three actions reinforce each other. The third is only defensible because the first two exist.
    - every action carries a named risk.
  - Mutual support is what separates coherent action from a backlog.
  - Named risk is what keeps a direction from hardening into a plan before anyone tested it.
- 🎨 Visual hint: One tall card divided into five labelled bands, --sienna headers with --ink body text, deliberately dense and small: the point is the shape of a whole brief, not readability of every word. A thin bracket down the left edge spanning all five, labelled "one page."

## 5. Part 4 · The Defence

---

### STATEMENT · Part intro
- Kicker: Part 4
- Title: THE DEFENCE
- On-slide: nothing else
- Speaker notes:
  - They walk in with `problem-brief.md` finished: diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent actions with named risks, non-goals, target state.
  - Nothing new gets built from here. Everything in this part uses what Parts 1 through 3 already produced.
  - **The shift:** up to now the brief was written. From here it gets argued with.
  - Same split named at the open, back again: not "how do you know," but "why not the other one."
- 🎨 Visual hint: Full-bleed --ink. "Part 4" small muted bottom-left.

### NUMBERED · The three questions that actually come
- Kicker: Part 4 · Prepare these
- Title: Prepare the three\nquestions, not the\n*whole deck.*
- On-slide: Header strip: "First, the readout. Pitch structure: implication headline → evidence → recommended decision, raw notes in an appendix." Then 3 items:
  - **"Why not the courier chat?"** → "It rests on one interview out of three, we've said so on the map, and it's named as a non-goal rather than forgotten."
  - **"Can we do both?"** → "That's the non-goals not being believed yet. Restate the trade, not the plan."
  - **"How big is this?"** → "We'll size it once we've explored the options. I'd rather size three real ones than guess at one."
- Speaker notes:
  - **First, the readout gets drafted, for the first time this quarter.** Last session held it back on purpose: a readout built on a hypothesis is an opinion with a template around it. Now there's a validated map, an insight and a finished brief behind it.
  - The pitch structure it uses carries over unchanged from Audit & Desk Research: implication headline, then the evidence, then the recommended decision, raw notes in an appendix.
  - **Then prepare for what actually gets asked.** None of the three questions below gets answered with more evidence, which is the real change from every pitch before this one.
  - They're answered with the rejection reason written down in Build 2 and the non-goals written down in Build 3. That's why both got written rather than left to memory.
  - **On the sizing question specifically:** they deliberately haven't sized it. Effort belongs to a solution, and no solution is chosen yet. Inventing a number here to sound decisive commits the team to their own first idea, so say that out loud if it comes up.
- 🎨 Visual hint: A thin header band naming the three-part pitch structure. Below it, three compact quotation-mark rows stacked, not two large blocks. Question in --ink, answer beneath it in --sienna, sized to let all three sit without crowding.

### MILESTONE · Defend
- Kicker: Practice
- Num: 11 min
- Title: Defend\nthe bet
- Prompt: Replay the decision you lost, from prep. Pitch this one. Take the pushback.
- On-slide: nothing else
- Speaker notes:
  - Activity 6. Timings: 2 replay · 3 pitch · 3 pushback · 3 coaching.
  - **"The decision you lost" is the overruled call they brought from prep** — the same one used as the example on the "bad strategy" slide back in Part 3. It gets replayed here as a drill, not just cited.
  - Pushback order: why not the other one · can we do both · how big is this · one hostile question from their own non-goals.
  - **Open with the replay.** Argue the lost decision as it happened, then again with today's brief.
  - Have them name the difference out loud: preference then, diagnosis plus policy plus a named trade now.
  - Mentor plays the sceptical stakeholder for the pushback round.
  - **The coaching is the payoff:** which answer leaned on evidence when it needed a stated trade-off, and which non-goal collapsed on first contact.

## 6. Close

---

### STATEMENT · Assignment
- Kicker: Assignment
- Title: Take it to the room.\nThen log what happened\nto the *non-goals.*
- On-slide: 5 short lines: "Finish the readout, render the brief as a one-pager" / "**Co-review with your PM and an engineer first**" / "Present to the same stakeholders" / "Log which non-goals survived" / "Update the brief with what you conceded, and why"
- Speaker notes:
  - **The real success check, a week later:** can a stakeholder restate the diagnosis in their own words?
  - If they restate your solution instead, the diagnosis wasn't what you actually communicated.
  - If they came in with 2 interviews, the extra conversation is part of this assignment too.
  - **The co-review is not for approval, it's for authorship.** Outcome agreed as a shared target, feasibility read on the directions.
  - A strategy written alone and defended alone is a designer's artifact. The one that ships is already owned by the trio before it reaches the room.
  - **The brief isn't done at the pitch.** Whatever the room changed gets written back in, with the concession named and why it was worth making.
- 🎨 Visual hint: Five numbered lines, minimal, with the third weighted in --sienna as the one that matters most.
