Overview

The biggest mistake in UX isn't bad design. It's designing for the wrong person. This session challenges one core assumption students carry into every project: that they already understand their users because they've read the brief, used the product, or worked in the industry for years. They don't.

The session is built around a mindset shift: you are not your user. From that foundation, students learn four connected practices that build toward a real user interview. First, friction metrics: a structured way to identify where users struggle in an existing experience. Second, assumption mapping: a method for surfacing what the team believes without evidence, before talking to users. Third, research questions: how to turn unvalidated assumptions into a purposeful interview guide. Fourth, user interview fundamentals: the behaviours that separate a useful interview from a conversation that confirms what you already think.

Each practice section includes a short in-class activity and uses AI tools to speed up the generation phase. The session closes with two assignments: conducting a 60-minute user interview and running a structured assumption map with confidence ratings.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, students will be able to:

  1. Identify friction in an existing experience using three friction types: cognitive, interaction, and emotional
  2. Categorise team assumptions by type (user, problem, solution, business) and write them in a testable format
  3. Rank assumptions on an importance-vs-evidence matrix to decide which to test first
  4. Convert an assumption into a focused research question and a set of 5–8 interview questions
  5. Apply user interview fundamentals: asking open questions, following the story, and listening more than speaking
  6. Use an AI tool to accelerate the preparation phase (assumption writing, interview question generation) while critically evaluating what it gets wrong

Core Content

The Mindset Shift: You Are Not Your User

The most important thing to remember when doing user research: you are not your user.

You've read the brief. You know the product category. You may have been in this industry for years. None of that makes you a reliable proxy for the people who actually use the product, and the gap between what you assume and what users actually experience is often where the most important design insights live.

The goal of this session is to build the habits that close that gap. Not by doing more research, but by doing targeted research: knowing exactly what to look for, what you believe without evidence, and what questions to ask.

Part 1: Friction Metrics

What is a friction metric?

A friction metric is a measurable indicator of where users struggle in an experience. It quantifies the cost of using a product at specific points in the journey, so design decisions can be grounded in evidence about real user difficulty, not team opinion.

There are three types of friction:

Cognitive friction: Mental effort required to understand or make a decision. Examples: unclear navigation labels, too many choices at once, jargon in UI copy, inconsistent patterns between screens. How to spot it: Users pause, re-read, ask "what does this mean?", or make the wrong selection on first attempt.

Interaction friction: Physical effort required to complete an action. Examples: small tap targets, too many steps to complete a task, form fields that reset on error, required information the user doesn't have to hand. How to spot it: Users tap wrong elements, re-enter the same data, abandon mid-flow, or take more steps than necessary to complete a task.

Emotional friction: Anxiety, confusion, or frustration caused by the experience. Examples: unclear error messages, trust issues at payment screens, fear of making an irreversible mistake, feeling overwhelmed by options. How to spot it: Users hesitate, express doubt verbally ("is this safe?"), abandon high-stakes flows, or give up and call support instead.

When to run a friction analysis

Run a friction analysis at the start of any project involving an existing product, or before any major redesign. It gives your team a shared, evidence-based picture of where the current experience is failing, before any solution is proposed. Without it, team members often disagree about where the real problems are because everyone is working from different anecdotes and intuitions.

Why it matters

Friction analysis makes the invisible visible. Every team member has an opinion about what's broken. A friction analysis makes those opinions testable. It also creates a baseline: once you've measured friction in the current state, you can measure whether the proposed design actually reduces it.

How to run one

Use a friction analysis table with two views:

Current Experience Friction Table (analyse what exists today):

Screen or step User action Friction type Friction detail Severity (1–5)
Ordering and checkout Choose a delivery window Cognitive Delivery window options aren't explained, users don't know if "ASAP" means 15 minutes or 45 3
Ordering and checkout Enter delivery address Interaction Apartment or unit number requirement not shown until after the order fails to submit 4
Waiting for delivery Track the order Emotional No indication of what happens if the ETA slips, no proactive alert, no way to reach the courier 5

Proposed Experience Friction Table (evaluate your proposed design against the same steps):

Screen or step User action Proposed change Friction type reduced Notes
Ordering and checkout Choose a delivery window Added inline explanation of what each window means Cognitive Eliminates the "what does this mean?" moment
Ordering and checkout Enter delivery address Show apartment or unit number requirement before the address field Interaction Reduces failed submissions and re-entries

Real case: NAB Simple Home Loan

A home loan application at NAB's prior experience involved 27 separate taps to complete. Not 27 screens — 27 taps on a single interaction model. A friction analysis of the current state identified the specific interaction frictions at each step. The redesigned flow reduced friction at the identified points. Result: a 60% reduction in measured friction. The improvement didn't come from visual redesign — it came from identifying the specific friction types and removing them systematically.

Part 2: Assumptions

What is an assumption?

An assumption is anything your team believes about users, problems, solutions, or the business, that you haven't yet proven with evidence.

Every design brief contains assumptions. Every sprint planning session is full of them. The problem isn't having assumptions. It's not knowing which ones are true and which ones could kill the product if wrong.

Full framework: The four assumption types, the WHO/WHAT/WHY/SIGNAL formula, and the Importance × Evidence matrix now live in Library/frameworks/assumption-map/framework-assumption-map.md: teach directly from that file. It's the single source of truth for this framework; keep this section as the session-specific application only.

Why this session runs it before the interview

An interview without a target produces pleasant conversation, not evidence. Running the Assumption Map before Part 3 (Research Questions) means students walk into their interview prep already knowing which 3–5 beliefs matter most and have the least evidence: those become the interview's actual agenda. The same map gets revisited and updated after the interview, in Assignment 2.

When to run an assumption map

Run it at the start of a project (before any research begins), when a project has stalled and the team is disagreeing about direction, when a stakeholder changes the brief, when a prototype test produces unexpected results, and after any major user research synthesis session. Full list and reasoning in the framework doc.

Part 3: Research Questions

What are research questions?

A research question is a specific, investigable question that, when answered, will either confirm or refute one of your assumptions. Research questions are not the same as interview questions: a research question defines what you need to learn; interview questions are the prompts you use to find the answer in a conversation.

The chain works like this: Assumption → Research question → 5–8 interview questions

How to write a research question

What: A focused, neutral question about behaviour, attitude, or context, not about your solution. How: Not too broad ("How do users feel about banking?") and not too narrow ("Do users prefer blue or green CTA buttons?"). Aimed at understanding reality, not confirming a preference. When: Written before any interview, before any design work, and before any solution is presented to users.

Why research questions matter: Without them, interviews drift. The conversation follows wherever the facilitator's natural curiosity leads, often toward solution feedback rather than problem understanding. Research questions keep the interview anchored to what the team actually needs to learn.

Good vs bad research questions

Bad research questions:

  • "What do users think of our new checkout redesign?" This is feedback-fishing, not research.
  • "Do users prefer the current or proposed navigation?" This is preference testing, not problem understanding.
  • "Would users use a feature that lets them do X?" Hypothetical behaviour questions produce unreliable answers.

Good research questions:

  • "How do users currently manage [specific task] without our product?"
  • "What causes users to stop mid-flow in a checkout process?"
  • "What information do users look for before committing to a plan?"

Real case: Urban Farming research A team researching urban farming practices came in with the assumption that the main barrier to entry was space and equipment cost. Their research question: "What prevents people who are interested in urban farming from getting started?" After interviewing 6 participants, the main barrier turned out to be knowledge and confidence, not cost or space. The product direction changed entirely because the research question was broad enough to capture the real answer.

From assumption to interview guide

Assumption Research question Interview questions
"Users struggle to find the right plan because there are too many options." "How do users currently navigate plan selection decisions?" "Walk me through the last time you compared two plans for any service. What did you do first?" / "What made it easy or hard?" / "How did you know you'd made the right choice?"
"Users are comfortable submitting documents via mobile." "What is users' experience with document-heavy tasks on mobile?" "Tell me about a time you had to upload something important on your phone." / "What made you feel confident or nervous about it?"

Part 4: User Interview Fundamentals

The five fundamentals

Good user interviews are a discipline, not just a skill. These five behaviours separate interviews that generate real insight from interviews that confirm what you already think:

1. Ask open questions Open questions ("Tell me about a time when...") invite stories. Closed questions ("Do you prefer X or Y?") invite yes/no answers. Closed questions feel efficient but produce data that can't be acted on. The single most important interviewing skill is resisting the urge to close.

2. Follow the story When a user says something unexpected or interesting, stop and follow it, even if it's off your script. The most important insights usually come from moments the interviewer didn't anticipate. Your script is a safety net, not a constraint.

3. Stay curious Approach every answer as though you've never heard this situation before, even if you've heard five identical answers in the last hour. Curiosity changes your body language, your tone, and the questions you follow up with. Users can tell whether you're genuinely interested or just ticking boxes.

4. Listen more than you speak If you're talking more than 20% of the time, something is wrong. Interviewers often fill silence with suggestions, summaries, or agreement noises that subtly guide the user's next answer. Silence is productive. Let it breathe.

5. Observe, don't just hear Watch what the user does as well as what they say. Hesitations, re-reads, and physical reactions are data. If a user says "it's fine" but pauses for 5 seconds before pressing a button, the pause is the insight, not the "fine."

The golden rule

The goal of a user interview is not to get the user to validate your ideas. It is to understand their reality. The moment you start presenting your solution or asking "would you use this?", you've stopped doing user research.

How AI tools help at this stage

AI tools can accelerate four specific tasks in the research preparation phase:

  1. Generating assumptions from a brief: Paste your brief and ask the AI to generate a list of testable assumptions across all four types. Review critically; the AI will miss context you know.
  2. Writing assumption statements: Use the formula ("We believe [WHO] will [WHAT] because [WHY]. We'll know when [SIGNAL]") as a prompt template. AI is fast at generating the structure; your judgment determines which ones are actually important.
  3. Drafting interview questions: Give the AI your research question and ask for 8–10 open-ended interview questions. Expect to cut at least half; AI tends to generate repetitive questions that circle the same point.
  4. Synthesising notes after interviews: Paste your interview notes and ask for themes. Useful as a first pass, but always return to the raw notes before drawing conclusions.

The limit of AI in research: it cannot assess which assumptions matter most for your specific project, it cannot evaluate whether an interview question is leading or neutral in your context, and it cannot replace the judgment that comes from sitting in a room with a real user.

Activities

🔍 Activity: A Simple Friction Review

Type: In-class · FigJam or Figma Time: 10 min Format: Solo, using your current screen flows

Open your screen flows from Assignment 3. You'll identify friction in your current experience using the three friction types.

Instructions:

  1. (3 min) Choose one key flow from your screen capture (e.g. onboarding, checkout, core task). Open it in FigJam or Figma.

  2. (5 min) For each major step in the flow, add a sticky note identifying one friction. Use a colour code:

    • 🟡 Yellow = Cognitive friction (understanding / decision difficulty)
    • 🔴 Red = Interaction friction (physical effort / too many steps)
    • 🔵 Blue = Emotional friction (anxiety / confusion / frustration)

Write each sticky in this format: "[User action] causes [friction type] because [specific issue]."

  1. (2 min) Share back: your single highest-severity friction point and why you rated it that way.

💡 Activity: Create Assumptions with AI

Type: In-class · AI chat tool + FigJam or Notion Time: 10 min Format: Solo, then small group share

Generate a set of assumptions for your project, then map them by importance and evidence.

Instructions:

  1. (3 min) Open your AI tool and paste this prompt:

"I'm a UX designer working on [project description]. My users are [who they are]. Generate 10 assumptions for this project, covering all four types: user assumptions, problem assumptions, solution assumptions, and business assumptions. Write each one in this format: 'We believe [WHO] will [WHAT] because [WHY]. We'll know when [SIGNAL].'"

  1. (4 min) Read the output. Select 4–5 assumptions that feel most relevant. Open your Assumption Map template in FigJam and place each assumption in the correct quadrant:

    • High importance + low evidence → top-left (test these first)
    • High importance + high evidence → top-right
    • Low importance → bottom half

  2. (3 min) Share back: which assumption lands in the top-left quadrant? Why is it high-stakes and unproven?

Critical check: Review the AI output carefully. Did the AI make your users sound too generic? Did it miss a key user segment, constraint, or competitor behaviour that you know about? Edit at least 2 of the AI-generated assumptions to make them more specific to your real project context.

📋 Activity: Prepare Research Questions

Type: In-class · AI chat tool + Notion Time: 10 min Format: Solo

Turn your top-priority assumption into a research question and a set of interview questions.

Instructions:

  1. (1 min) Take your highest-priority assumption (top-left quadrant from the mapping activity).

  2. (2 min) Write one research question for it. Use the format: "How / What / Why do users [behaviour or attitude]?" Keep it neutral, no solution language.

  3. (5 min) Open your AI tool and paste this prompt:

"I'm a UX designer preparing for a user interview. My research question is: [paste your research question]. Generate 8 open-ended interview questions that would help me answer this research question. Each question should invite a story, not a yes/no answer. Avoid hypothetical questions ('would you ever...?'). Start each question with Tell me, Walk me through, What, How, or Why."

  1. (2 min) Review the output. Cut any questions that are repetitive or leading. You should end up with 5–6 strong questions.

AI in Practice

🤖 Try these prompts

For assumption generation:

"I'm a UX designer working on [project]. Generate 10 assumptions across 4 types: user, problem, solution, and business. For each, use the format: 'We believe [WHO] will [WHAT] because [WHY]. We'll know when [SIGNAL].'"

For interview question generation:

"My research question is: [paste it]. Generate 8 open-ended interview questions. Avoid hypotheticals. Start each with Tell me, Walk me through, What, How, or Why."

For synthesis after interviews:

"Here are my interview notes from 3 user sessions: [paste notes]. Identify the 3 most common themes across all three sessions. For each theme, quote one specific thing a user said that illustrates it. Then list 2 assumptions that these sessions either confirm or refute."

🧠 Critical thinking

After using AI for assumption or question generation:

  • Which assumptions did the AI generate that you wouldn't have thought of? Are any of them genuinely testable?
  • Which AI-generated assumptions are too generic to be useful for your specific project?
  • Did the AI produce any leading interview questions, ones that hint at an expected answer? Rewrite them.

Assessment

Quick knowledge check

Run verbally at the close of the assumptions section (2–3 min):

  1. Name the 3 types of friction. Give one example of each from a product you use.
  2. A team says: "We already know our users well; we don't need to map assumptions." What's the risk in that?
  3. What's the difference between a research question and an interview question?
  4. A user says "Yeah, this looks fine" in an interview. What do you do next?

Assignments

Assignment 1: Conduct a User Interview

Due: Before next session Time estimate: 60–90 min preparation + interview Type: Research · Real conversation

Run one 60-minute user interview with a real user of your project. This is the most important deliverable in the course so far.

Before the interview:

  1. Choose a participant: a real user or someone who fits your primary user description. Not a friend, colleague, or family member who already knows your project.
  2. Use the research questions and interview questions from the in-class activity as your guide.
  3. Brief the participant: explain the purpose (understanding their experience), the format (60 minutes, questions about their experience, no right or wrong answers), and whether you'll record (get consent first).
  4. Set up a notes document: timestamp, participant pseudonym, direct quotes marked in quotation marks, observations noted separately from what was said.

During the interview:

  1. Open with a warm-up: "Tell me a bit about yourself and your role" or "Walk me through your typical week using [relevant product or task]."
  2. Ask your prepared questions. Follow any answer that surprises you.
  3. End with: "Is there anything I didn't ask that you think would be helpful for me to understand?"

After the interview:

  1. Write up your notes within 2 hours: memory fades quickly.
  2. Identify your 3 most surprising findings. What did you learn that you didn't already know?
  3. Note which assumptions were confirmed, which were refuted, and which need more data.

Deliverable: Interview notes document + reflection paragraph (what surprised you, what it means for your project). Share with your mentor before the next session.

AI in Practice: "Here are my interview notes: [paste notes]. What are the 3 most significant things this user told me? For each, tell me: which assumption it confirms or refutes, and what design implication it suggests."

Assignment 2: Assumption Mapping

Due: Before next session Time estimate: 45–60 min Type: Analysis · FigJam or Miro

Build a full assumption map for your project and rate your confidence in each assumption.

  1. List at least 10 assumptions across all four types (user, problem, solution, business). Use the formula: "We believe [WHO] will [WHAT] because [WHY]. We'll know when [SIGNAL]."
  2. Place each assumption on the Assumption Map: importance (y-axis) vs evidence (x-axis).
  3. Add a confidence level to each assumption: Unproven (no evidence), Partially validated (some indirect signals), or Validated (direct evidence from research).
  4. Identify the 3 assumptions you most need to test. For each one, write one research question.
  5. After your user interview (Assignment 1), return to the map and update the confidence levels based on what you learned.

Deliverable: FigJam or Miro link with your Assumption Map. Come ready to discuss in the next session.

AI in Practice: "I'm a UX designer working on [project]. Generate 10 assumptions across all four types. For each assumption, suggest: 1 research question that would help test it, and whether desk research, a user interview, or analytics data would be the best way to get the answer."

Overview

Audit & Desk Research left the mentee with evidence: a heuristic audit, a desk research pass, an updated Assumption Map, and a first attempt at insight statements. What it didn't answer is why any of it matters enough to the person living it. Evidence without motivation is what makes a stakeholder readout sound like a bug list instead of a reason to act.

This session closes that gap with Jobs to Be Done: the framework for understanding what a person is actually trying to accomplish, not who they demographically are. It's entirely new content for this audience, so it gets real teaching time, not a reference link. The session then runs the same discipline the mentee has already learned (target a belief, gather evidence, compare), but this time the belief being tested is the business's own assumption about what job the customer is hiring the product for. A short stakeholder check-in surfaces that assumption, using the Assumption Map skill the mentee already has rather than a new technique. A JTBD-informed customer interview, run more than once, tests it against what the customer actually says. The gap between assumed and actual is what the session builds into a working artifact.

The session opens with the stakeholders' belief and closes by turning the validated job into a JTBD map: stages across the top, evidence and pain stacked underneath, the 🔥 focus area marked where negative experience concentrates. Framing that map into a pitched insight (the three-leg formula: observation, implication, recommended decision) is next session's work. Problem Definition & Strategy picks up from exactly this map.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, mentees will be able to:

  1. Apply the JTBD framework (the three job layers, the job statement formula, and the "so that" test for telling a job from an action) to build a stage-by-stage map of candidate job hypotheses for your own project, including where a single stage carries more than one live job
  2. Run a stakeholder check-in that surfaces what the business assumes the customer's job is at each stage, starting from your own provisional guess and pointing the Assumption Map at one specific question rather than learning a new method
  3. Conduct a JTBD-informed customer interview using the switch-interview timeline, across enough conversations to see a pattern rather than rely on one
  4. Derive the actual job statement from that pattern, locate it on the stage map, and compare it against the stakeholder-assumed placement to find the gap between them
  5. Build a JTBD map that turns the validated job into a working artifact: stages across the top, JTBD and subtasks per stage, expectation and positive/negative experience stickies, an opportunity for each pain point, and a marked 🔥 focus area
  6. Hand off the map as the input the next session builds its insight and opportunity work on top of, rather than pitching a finding today

Success Check

  • The mentee can point to a specific gap between what a stakeholder assumed and what a customer actually said, not a vague "users want more" statement.
  • The mentee can produce a JTBD map with every stage filled in for at least one real user, the 🔥 focus area marked, and can say out loud how many interviews the actual job is based on.

Core Content

Opening · From Data to Decisions

Last session produced evidence: heuristic violations, comparator patterns, an updated Assumption Map. Evidence answers "what's broken." It doesn't answer "why does this matter enough to the person experiencing it" or "why should a stakeholder change a decision because of this." Those are two different failures, and this session is built to close both: motivation first, persuasion second.

Part 1 · Jobs to Be Done

What JTBD is

People don't hire a product because of who they are. They hire it to make progress on something specific, in a specific circumstance. Jobs to Be Done is the framework for finding that circumstance and that motivation, not a demographic description of the person.

Full framework (the three job types, the job statement formula, the switch-interview technique, and how to derive an actual job from a pattern) lives in Library/frameworks/jtbd/framework-jtbd.md. Teach directly from that file. What follows here is the condensed version plus this session's specific application.

Quick recap for the room:

Type Covers
Functional The practical task being accomplished
Emotional How the person wants to feel, or stop feeling
Social How the person wants to be seen by others

Formula: "When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome]."

Why it's a hypothesis, not a finding

A job statement written before talking to anyone is exactly as proven as an entry on the Assumption Map before it's tested, which is to say, not at all. Call it a candidate job hypothesis until a pattern across real interviews confirms it. This distinction matters more here than almost anywhere else in the curriculum: JTBD sounds authoritative when it's well-formatted, and that confidence is easy to mistake for evidence.

JTBD vs. persona

The fastest way to check a job statement: does it start with "when [situation]" or does it quietly start with "as a [type of person]"? The second one is a persona wearing JTBD language. Persona answers who; job answers why, in this specific circumstance; the same person can be hiring different jobs in different circumstances.

What JTBD looks like in action · breaking a job into stages

A job statement explains why someone is motivated. It doesn't say where in their process that motivation actually shows up, or how it shifts along the way; a recommendation is hard to scope without knowing which stage it belongs to. Break the job into its own stages, in your own words (however many the project actually has, typically 3 to 6, not a fixed universal count), and write a job statement for each stage.

What actually defines a stage boundary. A stage isn't an arbitrary label pulled from a generic funnel. It's exactly the circumstance from the job statement: "when [circumstance], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome]." As long as the circumstance holds, it's the same stage and the same job. The moment the circumstance changes enough that the motivation changes with it, that's a new stage, and it needs its own job statement. That's the test for where to cut a boundary: did the circumstance change, not did time pass.

Telling a job from an action: the "so that" test. JTBD phrasing collapses three different things into what sounds like one: the trigger (why someone entered the stage), the circumstance (what's true while they're in it), and the job (the underlying why of what they do inside it). Give the room a test: for anything that sounds like a job, ask "so that...?" If you can still add something new after it, you haven't reached the real job yet; you're still describing the action. Run it live: "enter delivery address," so that... the courier actually finds me. That's the real job; everything before it was still the action. A second example to have ready: "confirm order details," so that... I trust what I'm paying for.

One stage can carry more than one live job. A stage isn't guaranteed to reduce to a single job statement. Real example: food delivery, the checkout stage. Three genuine jobs live inside the same circumstance at once: confirm the order is right before paying, trust the payment is safe, and know when it'll arrive. Same circumstance the whole time (I'm checking out), but three different anxieties are live, so three job statements, not one. Don't force a stage into a single flat sentence if the evidence doesn't support it.

Applied to the food-delivery job used throughout this session: four stages, four genuinely different situations: browsing, ordering and checkout, waiting for delivery, receiving the order. The actual job ("let me stop worrying it's not coming") sits specifically at stage 3, waiting for delivery, not spread evenly across the whole thing. The job statement says that matters. The stage breakdown says exactly where. (The framework file's own worked example uses a different case, a home-loan application; full detail and its worked table: Library/frameworks/jtbd/framework-jtbd.md.)

This comes back in Part 4: once the actual job is derived from real interviews, it gets broken into its own stages to scope where the map and its focus area actually belong.

Part 2 · Point Your Assumption Map at the Customer's Job

Why this stage exists. Everything on the map so far is what the team believes and what the business believes; nobody's confirmed any of it is true yet. The people funding and shipping the work carry their own belief about the customer, untested, the same way the mentee's own stage-map hypotheses are untested. Benefit: this surfaces where the business's belief diverges from what the mentee is finding, before that gap becomes a fight at the readout. Outcome: a marked-up stage map showing assumed vs. actual, which feeds straight into the interview and the map.

This isn't a second interview method to learn. It's the Assumption Map skill from Audit & Desk Research, aimed at one specific question: what job does the business believe the customer is hiring us for, and why do they believe that?

The switch-interview technique from Part 1 is built to reconstruct a real customer's decision journey; it doesn't transfer to a stakeholder who never switched to anything. Running it on an internal conversation would be a category error, not just a shorter version of the same thing. What a stakeholder conversation actually produces is an assumption, captured exactly the way the Assumption Map already teaches: WHO/WHAT/WHY/SIGNAL, sorted by importance and evidence.

Placing the assumed job on the stage map

Before the real check-in happens, place a provisional guess of the stakeholder's belief onto the stage map built in Part 1: which stage do they likely think the job matters most at, and does that match where the mentee's own hypotheses point? This isn't the confirmed answer yet, it's a rehearsal: a visible, specific gap to go test, instead of walking into the meeting with an open-ended "what do you think the job is." The stage where the guess and the hypothesis diverge most is the one worth listening for specifically once the real conversation happens.

Questions to bring into the meeting:

  • "What job do you think the customer is doing here?"
  • "Why do you believe that?"
  • "What would change your mind?"

Practical note: this doesn't need a booked meeting of its own. Fold it into a 1:1, a standup, a check-in that's already on the calendar. Getting dedicated time with a PM or business owner inside the same window as everything else in this assignment is often the harder scheduling problem: solve it by attaching to something that already exists, not by adding a new invite.

Part 3 · The JTBD-Informed Customer Interview

Why this stage exists. The stage map is still a hypothesis. Time to test it: so far it's team hypotheses and stakeholder beliefs, no customer has confirmed any of it yet. This is the session's primary method: where the actual job gets tested against real behaviour, not assumed belief. Benefit: it will confirm or correct the map before anything gets built on top of a guess. Outcome by the end of Part 3: a validated (or revised) JTBD map, backed by real quotes, ready to hand to design.

The switch timeline

Structure the conversation around the timeline of the decision, not a list of open questions about the product. Full detail in the framework file; the five stages: first thought, passive looking, active looking, deciding, using. Each stage has its own prompt: "when did you first start thinking about this," "what were you doing instead before you looked for something new," "what made today the day you actually went looking," "what almost stopped you," "is it doing what you hoped."

Scoping check · does this project even have a switch moment?

The switch-interview technique assumes a real decision point: someone was doing something else, then chose this instead. That fits a project with a genuine adoption or application moment: applying for a loan, opening an account, switching providers. It fits far less cleanly onto a workflow feature inside a process someone's already committed to, where there's no "switch," only a task. Before drafting an interview guide, name which one this project actually is. If there's no switch moment, adapt the timeline to task initiation instead: first thought about needing to do the task, what they tried first, what made them look for a better way, what they chose, whether it worked.

Why more than one

One conversation is a story about one person on one day. The job is what repeats. Two to three interviews showing the same circumstance-motivation-outcome shape is the minimum bar before treating it as an actual job rather than an anecdote; this is the same discipline as the desk research minimum from last session (2–3 comparators, not one).

Real example. Three interviews, same stage, two agree: "I kept refreshing the tracking screen because I was scared it had just stopped." "I checked the map every few minutes, worried the driver had given up." The third interview tells a genuinely different story: wanting a person on the line, not a tracking screen. That third one doesn't get promoted to "the job" just because it's vivid or recent; two matching accounts outweigh one striking one.

What the interview leaves you with

Once the interview pattern is derived, one row on the map is fully filled in: stage, hypothesis (Part 1), assumed job (Part 2), actual job (Part 3), and the gap between assumed and actual. Worked example: assumed "show me my order is on the way" vs. actual "let me stop fearing it's not coming," both located at the waiting-for-delivery stage. That gap is exactly what Part 4 turns into a working artifact.

Part 4 · Mapping the Job

Why this stage exists. The validated job doesn't stay a sentence. It becomes a visual map, because that's the format a design or delivery team can actually act on, and the format the next two lessons build directly on top of: customer journey mapping and service blueprinting both start from exactly this map. Skipping this step means re-deriving the same structure twice.

Set the foundation

Before filling in any stage, write down what's already known at the top of the map. This is a recap, not new teaching; every field already exists somewhere in the mentee's project.

  • Business objective: what the stakeholder check-in said actually matters to the business
  • Target user context: the circumstance from the job statement, who and when
  • Goals & KPIs: whatever the team already measures success by

The only new move is writing them at the top of the map so anyone reading it shares the same frame before they hit the first stage.

Anatomy of the map

Read it left to right. Stages across the top are the confirmed stages from Part 3, unchanged. Each stage gets:

  • A JTBD + subtasks card: the job statement plus the concrete steps someone actually takes at that stage
  • A screenshot or flow slot, if the experience already exists
  • Sticky types stacked underneath: expectation (what the user assumes will happen), positive and negative experience (what helps versus what hinders), and opportunity (a potential fix for the negative one)

Multiple users or personas get their own row: same stages across the top, a different lived experience underneath.

Filled example

One real stage, filled in, using the food-delivery job. Stage: waiting for delivery. JTBD: "When my order is out for delivery, I want to know it's still coming, so I can stop fearing it's not." Subtask: check the tracking screen. Expectation: the app will warn me before I have to ask. Negative experience: the ETA slips with no explanation. Opportunity: push a proactive alert the moment the ETA changes, instead of a static countdown the user has to keep checking.

Find the focus area

Once every stage has its stickies filled in, scan the whole row for where negative experience stacks up highest: that's the 🔥 focus area. This isn't a new judgment call. It's the same stage the actual job pointed to back in Part 3, now visible on the map instead of buried in an interview note. Mark it: it should match what customers and stakeholders already told you, and it's the entry point for the next two lessons, customer journey mapping and service blueprinting.

Turning this map into a pitched insight, the three-leg formula of observation, implication, and recommended decision, and framing it for a room, is next session's work. Problem Definition & Strategy picks up from exactly this map.

Activities

🗺️ Activity · Build Your Stage Map

Type: In-class · Real audit findings + AI tool linked to your project folder Time: 9 min Format: Solo

  1. (2 min) Name the stages your project's job actually goes through, in your own words. Most projects land somewhere between 3 and 6. There's no fixed count or fixed vocabulary; name what's actually there.
  2. (5 min) Point your AI tool at your audit findings and Assumption Map, already saved in your project folder: no need to re-explain the project. Ask it to (1) generate the stages, and (2) draft one or more candidate JTBD hypotheses per stage, using this format: "When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome]", naming that a stage can hold more than one job. Output as jtbd-map.md, labelled draft.
  3. (2 min) Check each stage's hypothesis against two tests: does it start with "when [situation]" rather than "as a [type of person]"? And does it survive the "so that...?" test: if you can still add something new after it, it's still an action, not the job yet. Rewrite anything that fails either one.

Critical check: AI defaults to demographic language unless told not to. If a hypothesis reads like a persona with extra steps, that's the tell.

Deliverable: save the stage map to jtbd-map.md (markdown): one or more candidate JTBD hypotheses per stage, labelled draft, not yet validated.

🗣️ Activity · Prepare Your Stakeholder Check-in

Type: In-class · Stage map from the previous activity + Assumption Map template Time: 4 min Format: Solo

  1. (1 min) Place your own guess for each stage: which one does the stakeholder likely think the job matters most at, and does that match your own hypothesis? Circle the stage with the biggest gap.
  2. (1 min) Identify the meeting already on your calendar that this question can attach to.
  3. (2 min) Draft 2–3 questions for that meeting, aimed at the stage where step 1 showed the biggest gap: what job do they think the customer is hiring you for at that point, why they believe that, what would change their mind. Optionally, ask your AI tool to draft the check-in plan: a goal for the conversation, the 2–3 questions aimed at the stage with the biggest gap, what answer would confirm vs. break each one, and how to log it.

Deliverable: the check-in questions saved as a new entry on your existing assumption-map.md (markdown): same WHO/WHAT/WHY/SIGNAL formula, ready to log the real answer once the meeting happens.

📋 Activity · Draft Your JTBD-Informed Interview Guide

Type: In-class · AI tool linked to your project folder + framework file Time: 10 min Format: Solo

  1. (1 min) Name whether your project has a real switch moment or needs the task-initiation adaptation from Part 3.
  2. (2 min) Take your stage map. Write one prompt for each stage of the timeline (first thought, passive/active looking, deciding, using) in your own words.
  3. (5 min) Point your AI tool at your jtbd-map.md, already saved. Ask it to generate 8–10 interview questions along the switch timeline that test these specific stage hypotheses, not generic questions. Each should invite a story, not a yes/no answer.
  4. (2 min) Cut to your strongest 5–6. Flag any that are really asking someone to explain their own motivation directly; rewrite those as timeline questions instead.

Deliverable: save to interview-guide.md (markdown).

AI in Practice · Extend Your Audit Toolkit

Four reusable assets came out of Audit & Desk Research. This session adds a fifth, following the same rule: extend the existing file, don't regenerate it.

🤖 The fifth asset

jtbd-map.md: new today, markdown, living and append-only, same as assumption-map.md and competitor-pattern-log.md. Four parts, built in order across this session and the assignment: (1) the stage map: the project's own stages, one or more candidate JTBD hypotheses per stage, drafted first; (2) the stakeholder's assumed job, placed against those same stages as a provisional guess today, then replaced with the confirmed answer once the real check-in happens; (3) the actual job, derived post-interview from the pattern across real conversations, plus the gap against the assumed placement; (4) the full map itself, built as a Figma or FigJam (or Miro) board from this markdown source: every stage, JTBD + subtasks, the sticky types, and the marked 🔥 focus area. A "linked insight" field stays a placeholder this session; it gets filled in properly next session, once Problem Definition & Strategy teaches the three-leg formula it takes to turn a gap into something a stakeholder can act on.

🧠 Critical thinking

  • Where did your candidate job hypothesis survive contact with a real interview, and where did it turn out to be your own assumption in JTBD clothing?
  • After comparing assumed vs. actual: which was harder to get right, running the interview, or resisting the urge to call one good interview "the answer"?

✍️ Prompt engineering tip

Your AI tool already has this project's files. After each interview, save the raw notes into the folder and ask AI to cross-check them against jtbd-map.md: "Does this interview support, contradict, or complicate the stage hypotheses in my jtbd-map? Quote the specific line that makes you say so." Check after every interview, not just once you think you're done: it's cheaper to catch a hypothesis drifting after interview 1 than after interview 3. There's no need to re-paste anything each time; it's already sitting in the same folder.

⚖️ Ethics consideration

A well-formatted job statement or map is not the same as a validated one. A JTBD map built on genuine evidence and one built on confident AI phrasing can read identically on the board; the difference only shows up if someone asks how many people you actually talked to.

Assessment

Quick knowledge check

Run verbally at the close of Part 4 (2–3 min):

  1. What's the difference between a job statement and a persona?
  2. Why is a job statement written before any interview still just a hypothesis?
  3. A stakeholder says, "I already know why our customers do this." What's your response, using what you learned today?
  4. Your notes from one customer interview contain a great quote that perfectly proves your hypothesis. Can you present it to a stakeholder as a validated insight? Why or why not?
  5. Once every stage on the map has its stickies filled in, how do you decide where the 🔥 focus area goes?

Assignments

Assignment · From Assumed Job to a Working Map

Due: Before next session Time estimate: 3–4 hours, spread across 1–2 weeks to allow for real scheduling Type: Research + build · Your real project

  1. Run the stakeholder check-in inside the meeting you already identified. Log the assumed job on your Assumption Map, and replace your draft placement in jtbd-map.md with what they actually said.
  2. Conduct 2–3 JTBD-informed customer interviews using your interview guide. Not one: the pattern is the point. If a real scheduling blocker makes it truly impossible this cycle, say so explicitly and label the result "assumed" rather than "actual," since it's still coming from the stakeholder, not the customer.
  3. After each interview, check it against your stage-map hypotheses (see the AI in Practice prompt engineering tip). Derive the actual job statement from what repeats across all of them: the job is what at least 2 of 3 people said.
  4. Update your stage map in jtbd-map.md with what the real interviews actually showed: confirm, revise, or add stages where the draft from this session guessed wrong.
  5. Update jtbd-map.md: stage-by-stage hypotheses, confirmed stakeholder-assumed job, actual job, the gap, and the confirmed stage breakdown.
  6. Build the full JTBD map from Part 4's template: every stage, both users where relevant, JTBD + subtasks, every sticky type (expectation, positive, negative, opportunity), and the marked 🔥 focus area, as a board in Figma or FigJam (or Miro), plus the markdown source.

Deliverable: updated jtbd-map.md (markdown) and the JTBD map itself as a Figma or FigJam (or Miro) board. Share before the next session.

Draft the actual job statement from that pattern, and flag anything that only showed up in one interview so I don't treat it as confirmed."
